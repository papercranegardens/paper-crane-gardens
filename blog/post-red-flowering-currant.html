Why Red-flowering Currant Should Be in Every Portland Garden

Taylor Real
March 2026
5 min read

If there's one plant that captures the spirit of a Pacific Northwest spring more completely than any other, it's Ribes sanguineum — red-flowering currant. Before the cherry blossoms open, before the tulips push through, this understory shrub erupts into cascades of deep pink-red flowers that stop passersby cold and signal to pollinators that winter is finally over.

And yet, for all its drama, red-flowering currant is one of the easiest-going plants you can put in a Portland garden. It asks for almost nothing and gives back enormously.

A Plant That Works for You

Red-flowering currant is native to the Pacific Northwest — which means it has evolved over thousands of years to thrive in exactly our climate. It handles our wet winters, dry summers, and everything in between. Once established (typically after one to two seasons), it requires essentially zero supplemental irrigation. In our ecological maintenance practice, it's one of the plants we love to incorporate because it genuinely reduces the workload while elevating a garden's ecological value.

Here's what it does for your garden:

  • Blooms February through April — filling a critical gap when most other plants are dormant and early pollinators are desperate for food
  • Provides habitat and nesting material for birds throughout the season
  • Produces small blue-black berries in summer, beloved by cedar waxwings, robins, and other fruit-eating birds
  • Tolerates part shade to full sun, giving it remarkable versatility
  • Grows 5–12 feet tall and wide in an arching, graceful form that works in naturalistic and formal gardens alike

Where It Shines

In a Portland garden, red-flowering currant earns its keep in a few specific situations. It's spectacular against a dark fence or building where the flower clusters can really pop. It works beautifully as a loose hedge or screening shrub along a property line. And in a woodland garden — underplanted with sword fern, native bleeding heart, and trillium — it creates a layered native habitat planting that feels genuinely wild.

"The most rewarding thing about designing with native plants is watching the wildlife arrive that you didn't plant for. Put in red-flowering currant and within a season, you'll have hummingbirds you never had before."

Rufous hummingbirds time their northward spring migration specifically around the bloom time of red-flowering currant. It's not a coincidence — it's millions of years of co-evolution. When you plant this shrub, you're not just adding color. You're restoring a relationship.

Varieties Worth Knowing

The straight species is beautiful, but a few named selections are worth seeking out. 'King Edward VII' produces exceptionally deep crimson flowers. 'White Icicle' offers creamy white blooms for gardens that want softer color. 'Elk River Red' is a compact selection that tops out around six feet, useful for smaller spaces. Ask your local native plant nursery what they're growing — regional provenance matters for habitat value.

Care Notes

Red-flowering currant is remarkably low-maintenance once established. Prune lightly after flowering — never before, or you'll remove the coming year's flower buds. Remove any dead or crossing wood to maintain good air circulation and an open, graceful form. Avoid heavy feeding; this plant doesn't need it and excessive nitrogen will push leafy growth at the expense of flowers. Mulch around the base to retain moisture during our dry summers, especially in the first few years.

If you're starting a native garden in Portland, or if you have a spot that needs color in late winter and early spring, red-flowering currant is your answer. It's available at most native plant nurseries in the region, and at Paper Crane Gardens we incorporate it into nearly every ecological planting we design.
