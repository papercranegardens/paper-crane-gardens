What to Do in Your Pacific Northwest Garden in February

Taylor Real
October 2025
5 min read

February in the Pacific Northwest is a month of small promises. The light is perceptibly longer. Indian plum is beginning to leaf out along stream banks. Snowdrops are pushing through, and if you look carefully at the tips of red-flowering currant branches, you can see the tight bud clusters that will open into cascading pink flowers within weeks.

It's also one of the most productive months to be in the garden. The soil is workable — not frozen, but not yet dry enough to need irrigation. Many tasks that would stress plants later in the season can be accomplished now with minimal impact. Here's how we think about February in an ecological Portland garden.

Clean Up Winter Damage — Thoughtfully

Walk the garden and assess what the winter has done. Note any branches broken by ice or wind. Look for signs of frost damage on marginally hardy plants. Check that stakes and ties haven't damaged bark as plants have moved in winter storms.

But resist the urge for wholesale cleanup. The leaf litter and standing dead stems you see are doing important work. Overwintering moth larvae, native bee pupae, and beneficial insects are sheltering in exactly that material. Wait until daytime temperatures are consistently above 50°F — typically late March in the Portland area — before cleaning up perennial beds. By then, beneficial insects will have emerged.

What can come out now: truly dead wood on shrubs and trees, any diseased material that might spread fungal problems, and debris that has blown into paths and paved areas.

Pruning: What's Ready, What Isn't

February is excellent timing for dormant-season pruning of many trees and deciduous shrubs. With leaves off, you can see the architecture clearly — crossing branches, structural problems, deadwood. Fruit trees, roses, hydrangeas, and most deciduous shrubs can all be pruned now.

What not to prune in February:

  • Spring-blooming shrubs (forsythia, lilac, rhododendron, viburnum) — these have already set their flower buds; pruning now removes the flowers you've been waiting for
  • Ornamental grasses — leave these until the very end of February or into March, just before new growth emerges
  • Marginally hardy plants — wait until you see new growth and can assess what survived before cutting

Soil Care and Feeding

February is a good time to assess soil health and add amendments before the growing season begins. Top-dress beds with a one-to-two-inch layer of finished compost, keeping it away from the crowns of plants. This improves structure, adds slow-release nutrients, and encourages the soil biology that makes healthy plant growth possible.

"We think about soil like a bank account. Every time you add organic matter, you're making a deposit that will pay dividends all season. February is a great time to deposit."

If you have established beds with healthy organic matter, they may not need feeding at all. Healthy soil feeds healthy plants — the goal should be building the soil, not feeding the plants. Look at your plants and your soil before reaching for any product.

Planting Opportunities

February is excellent for planting bare-root trees, shrubs, and roses. Bare-root plants — available from specialty nurseries from January through March — are dug dormant and sold without soil around the roots. They're less expensive than container plants, establish quickly, and often outgrow containerized plants within a season or two. If you've been planning to add a fruit tree, a native shrub, or a climbing rose, now is when bare-root availability is at its best.

Container-grown natives — sword fern, native currants, Oregon grape, ferns and groundcovers — can also be planted now. The combination of soil moisture and cool temperatures is ideal for root establishment before the growing season begins.

What to Watch For

The end of February is when the Pacific Northwest garden truly begins to stir. Watch for the first emerging croziers of sword fern — hairy, tightly coiled fiddleheads pushing through the soil. Watch for buds swelling on maples and bigleaf maple. Listen for the first rufous hummingbird, which typically arrives in Portland in late February or early March, often on the day that red-flowering currant first opens.

This is the part of gardening that no amount of maintenance work can replicate: the sense of watching something wake up, knowing that your choices — what you planted, what you left standing, what you prepared — contributed to it. It's why we garden.
